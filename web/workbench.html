The workbench

A bench, a shelf unit of three bays along the whole back edge, one arm on a rail across the front, and twenty-one things. This is the world the demo happens in — a look at it, nothing more. Drag to turn the view, scroll to move in and out, and move the pointer over anything to put the spot on it.

Loading…

In this virtual world every object and every position is already known. No AI model works out what is on the bench or where it is. In a real room that half is genuinely hard, and that is what a vision-language model such as NVIDIA Cosmos is for.

We are building the other half: once the world is known, how does a person tell a robot which thing and which place?

What to look for

Five sets of the same thing
Four jars, two tins, two screwdrivers, two offcuts, and two tubes of glue that differ only by a cap. No word tells any set apart, so only pointing can. That is the whole demo, and it is what a real bench looks like.
A fourth jar
Behind the middle one, in the back row. From this view you cannot see it at all, and neither can the robot. Going up does not find it; drag the view round to one side and it appears.
Two tubes of glue
To the right of the jars: one capped, one open. They differ by a state, not a word.
The arm, and its rail
One arm that slides across the front. It cannot work closer in than 135 mm from itself, nor more than 100° off the way it faces, so a fixed arm drags a dead zone about with it wherever you put it. Sliding, it can always stand square in front of whatever it is reaching for — which is why every bay is reachable along its whole width, and two fixed arms could not manage that.
Picking things up
Keep the pointer on the thing and press P. The arm slides along its rail until it is standing where it can work, comes at the thing from above if it is on the bench or straight in from the front if it is under a board, and closes the jaws on it. Then point somewhere and press D. When it cannot, it says why. The keys are the point: a hand cannot keep pointing at something and click a button at the same time, and in the finished demo this is the space bar and a sentence.
A bottle of oil
On the right of the bench, 150 mm tall. It is taller than either bay is high, so asking for it to be put away has an honest answer: it cannot be.
The top board
Empty, and it stays empty. Reaching down onto anything much above 100 mm is not a pose this arm has, so the unit's lid is not a place it can use.

Every size here was measured

The geometry has one source, workbench/scene.py, and the physics scene is generated from it, so the shelf the arm reaches into and the shelf this page draws a spot on are the same shelf. The sizes rest on two measurements run against the real inverse kinematics: the widest thing the gripper holds through a lift and a move (76 mm on the jaws alone, 96 with the grip assist), and the reach ring at each height something here is gripped at. Reaching level into the bottom bay the arm cannot work closer in than 280 mm — which is what sets how far back the rail runs, because that distance has to swallow both rows of the shelf. Every thing here stands somewhere the arm can really work, and nothing stands in anything else's place — both are tested, not eyeballed.

Nothing drives anything yet: no physics, no voice, no model. The arms are in their resting pose, and everything is exactly where the numbers put it.
